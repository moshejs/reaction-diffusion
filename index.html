Two chemicals and one honest rule

Imagine a shallow dish coated with two dissolved chemicals, U and V. Three things happen everywhere at once. First, both chemicals diffuse — they spread out from where they're dense toward where they're thin, U roughly twice as fast as V. Second, they react: wherever a unit of U meets two units of V, all three become more V. So V is autocatalytic — it eats U to make copies of itself. Left alone, V would run away and consume everything.

Two more terms keep it honest. Fresh U is constantly fed in at a rate f, topping up the fuel. And V is constantly killed off — removed at a rate f+k. Feed too little or kill too much and V starves out to a blank field; feed too much and it floods. Between those extremes lies a narrow, fertile band where V can't quite win and can't quite lose — and that tension is where patterns live.

Why it doesn't just blur into mush

Diffusion, on its own, only ever smooths things out. The magic is the difference in diffusion speeds. V catalyzes itself locally, building a spot; but the U it consumes is replenished from farther away more slowly than V spreads, so a ring of exhausted, V-hostile territory forms around each spot. That's short-range activation, long-range inhibition — a spot that grows itself while forbidding its neighbours. Repeat it across the whole dish and you get evenly spaced dots, or, if the geometry favours it, stripes and labyrinths.

This is exactly the mechanism Alan Turing proposed in his 1952 paper The Chemical Basis of Morphogenesis — his last great idea before his death. He argued that an embryo, starting as a nearly uniform blob, could break its own symmetry into structure using nothing but reacting, diffusing morphogens. It was pure math decades ahead of the biology; only much later did researchers find real Turing-like systems setting the spacing of hair follicles, the ridges of your palate, the stripes on a fish.

The map you're standing on

The little feed × kill plot next to the sliders isn't decoration — it's the atlas. In 1993 John Pearson swept this exact plane and found it carved into more than a dozen distinct regimes, each with a Greek-letter name. The presets drop you onto the famous ones: Coral creeps outward in branching reefs; Mitosis grows spots that swell and split like dividing cells; Maze freezes into a labyrinth of even-width corridors; Fingerprint settles into looping ridges; Spots & worms can't decide and does both at once. Slide a few thousandths in either direction and the whole character of the field changes — the borders between regimes are that sharp.

Where it shows up

Once you've watched it run, you start seeing it everywhere: the spots on a leopard and the stripes on a zebrafish, the ridges inside a seashell, the branching of coral and lichen, the spacing of sand ripples, the pigment blotches on a pufferfish. Nature reruns this same short-range-yes, long-range-no bargain at wildly different scales. What you're painting on this page is a caricature of the real chemistry — but the family resemblance is the whole point.
